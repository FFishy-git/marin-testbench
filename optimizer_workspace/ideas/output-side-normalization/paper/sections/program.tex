\section{What would settle this}
\label{sec:program}

The results above leave one question open in a way that blocks all the others:
is the fixed-rate gain a geometry effect or a step-size effect? Until that is
answered, additional arms add precision to a quantity nobody is asking about.
This section lists the experiments we think are worth running, in priority
order, with what each decides and what it costs. The list is deliberately
short. Section~\ref{sec:nonprogram} says what we would \emph{not} run, which in
a study with this many degrees of freedom is the more useful half.

A full-budget run here is 2.68B tokens on eight A100s, about 1.7 hours of
wall-clock and roughly 13 GPU-hours. We quote costs in runs.

\subsection{Item 1 deserves to be first}

Of everything in Table~\ref{tab:program}, the norm-grafting control is the one
we most regret not having run. The concern it addresses is specific: a left
factor $\hat L$ with unit RMS does not fix the final direction's norm, because
it is applied after a finite Newton--Schulz map whose output is not an exact
polar factor, and the decay term is shaped separately. So an output-side arm may
simply be taking larger effective steps than the baseline at the same nominal
rate --- which Section~\ref{sec:tuning} shows is worth far more than any
geometry we tested.

Grafting separates the hypotheses in one run. Replace the candidate direction
$D$ by $D\,\|D_{\text{input}}\|_F/\|D\|_F$, where $D_{\text{input}}$ is the
baseline's direction for the same matrix at the same update, leaving everything
else alone. If the gain survives, the output moment is reorienting the update
and the geometry story stands. If it disappears, the effect is step size, and
items 2--5 can be skipped in favour of simply tuning the baseline. A
complementary cheap measurement, available from existing checkpoints with no
new runs, is the effective step ratio $\|\Delta W\|_F/\|W\|_F$ per arm: if the
candidates' ratios systematically exceed the baseline's, that is corroborating
evidence before the grafted run even starts.

\subsection{Mechanistic probes, at zero training cost}

No mechanistic evidence exists for \emph{why} output-side normalization helps.
That is the largest gap in this study, and closing most of it requires no new
training: the runs saved 21 distributed checkpoints each, including optimizer
moments and roots, so the following can be computed offline on reserved batches
at the 0\%, 10\%, 50\% and 100\% points of training.

\paragraph{Does the output moment carry anisotropy worth exploiting?}
Measure the top-to-mean eigenvalue ratio and leading energy share of $B$ per
projection and per depth, alongside the same quantities for $C$. If the
width-2048 output moments are nearly isotropic, the diagonal result of
Section~\ref{sec:freshness} needs no freshness explanation at all --- their
rotation would carry little information to begin with --- and that single
measurement would discriminate between our interpretation and the simpler one.
We regard this as the most informative probe per unit effort in the paper.

\paragraph{Does the Kronecker factorization hold here?}
This is the direct test of the motivation in Section~\ref{sec:background}. On a
reserved pool, form \emph{fresh} empirical moments
$\widehat C=N^{-1}\sum_j x_jx_j^{\Tr}$ and
$\widehat B=N^{-1}\sum_j \delta_j\delta_j^{\Tr}$ --- not training EMAs, and not
stale roots --- and for fixed unit-Frobenius directions $D$ compare the true
quadratic $N^{-1}\sum_j(\delta_j^{\Tr}Dx_j)^2$ against the factored
$\tr(\widehat B D\widehat C D^{\Tr})$. Agreement would license calling
$B\otimes C$ a curvature model here; disagreement would localize the failure to
cross-token terms, which can be inspected directly. Two disciplines matter:
rank the directions by both quantities and compare the rankings rather than the
magnitudes, and treat a correlation interval containing zero as no support for
the curvature account. Anisotropy alone does not establish it.

\paragraph{Does the finite map behave like the idealized one?}
Our measurements put the ten-step map at cosine $0.979$ to a partial polar
factor with relative error $0.211$ (Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}), so arguments
that assume $PP^{\Tr}=I$ do not transfer. Comparing exact SVD-polar against
$\Nten$ on small matrices, with explicit rank-deficiency conventions, would
quantify how far the arms' algebra departs from the derivation that motivated
them.

\subsection{What we would not run}
\label{sec:nonprogram}

Three large bodies of work are available and we think they should wait.

\paragraph{The full-model Newton--Schulz budget sweep.}
Fewer Newton--Schulz steps is a genuine efficiency lever: our numerical study
finds seven steps matching ten on cosine to a partial polar factor
($0.980$ against $0.979$) with 30\% fewer matrix products, and five steps still
at $0.951$. But the natural experiment reserves 54 full configurations across
three families, two operand precisions and three budgets. That is six times the
program in Table~\ref{tab:program} to answer a question orthogonal to whether
output-side normalization works. Newton--Schulz cost is shared by every arm
including the baseline, so the right first test is a three-point step-budget
comparison \emph{on the baseline alone}, where the result is cheaper to
interpret and benefits every arm if it holds. We would also not describe such a
change as an implementation speedup: five- and seven-step directions differ
from ten-step ones by relative errors of $0.41$ and $0.38$, so this is a
different optimizer, not a faster one.

\paragraph{The remaining survivor controls and information controls.}
A direction/decay factorial, a formula control in the style of published
two-sided methods, a dimension-capped input control, and the information
controls that re-apply observed eigenvalues in a randomized basis or shuffle
eigenvalues within the observed basis are all defensible, and the
randomized-basis control in particular is the clean way to ask whether the
eigenbasis or merely the spectrum carries the benefit. None of them changes the
decision currently in front of us, which is a cost decision. We would run the
randomized-basis control immediately \emph{after} item 3, if and only if a
tuned gain survives.

\paragraph{Scaling.}
Larger batches and larger models should wait for a tuned win at this scale.
The one larger-batch pair we have (Section~\ref{sec:replication}) gives a
smaller gain, and the pinned reference values for this recipe family show the
baseline-to-preconditioned gap \emph{growing} with batch size, which means a
larger batch changes what is being compared rather than simply amplifying it.
Scaling an unpriced effect multiplies the cost of not having priced it.

\subsection{Decision rules, set before the runs}

We state these in advance because the temptation to read a 0.002-nat
improvement as a win is exactly what the matched null and the tuning sweep
exist to resist.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Attribution.} A geometry claim is reported as candidate minus
  \emph{same-wave, same-rate} matched null, never as candidate minus baseline.
\item \textbf{Quality.} A quality claim requires the tuned, full-budget margin
  of item 3 to exceed the null artifact and to hold on the fresh seeds of item 5
  on the fixed external metric.
\item \textbf{Efficiency.} An efficiency claim requires a predeclared target
  from baseline-only pilots, a clock that starts at process launch and includes
  compilation, evaluation and checkpointing, and explicit censoring where a
  target is unreached. Nominal product counts, kernel microbenchmarks and
  retrospective crossings on completed histories do not substitute, and our own
  retrospective crossings (Section~\ref{sec:cost}) are reported as exploratory
  for this reason.
\item \textbf{Margins.} We decline to fix a numerical decision margin until
  item 5 provides a baseline seed-to-seed variability estimate for this recipe.
  The paired design makes within-seed differences precise, but no number we
  currently have bounds the marginal variance, so any threshold we named now
  would be arbitrary --- and the candidate effects, at $0.002$ to $0.007$ nats,
  are close enough to plausible noise floors that the order of operations
  matters.
\end{itemize}
