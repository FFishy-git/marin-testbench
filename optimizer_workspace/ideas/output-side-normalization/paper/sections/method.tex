\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Where the output statistics come from}

The transformer we train has 32 layers and seven two-dimensional block matrices
per layer --- separate $q,k,v,o$ attention projections and a SwiGLU
$\text{gate},\text{up},\text{down}$ triple --- for 224 matrices under the matrix
optimizer. Embeddings, the output head and RMSNorm weights stay under a fused
AdamW group and are untouched by every arm.

The two sides are not symmetric in how much sharing they permit, and this is
the first design consequence of using the output moment at all.
Table~\ref{tab:projections} shows why: $q,k,v$ consume the same activations and
can therefore share one input moment, as can $\text{gate},\text{up}$, so the
input side needs four statistics per layer. Every projection has its own output
cotangent and can share nothing, so the output side needs seven. Two of those
seven are at width 2048, against the input side's one.

Cotangents are sampled at the same 286 positions per sequence as the input
statistics, giving $N_t=36{,}608$ samples per update pooled across all
microbatches and all eight ranks, with EMA coefficient $\beta=0.938$ --- an
effective averaging window of about 16 updates. Raw FP32 sums and counts pool
\emph{before} the EMA, so the statistic is a globally pooled quantity and not a
per-rank one. Because any positive constant scale cancels from the trace
normalization in \eqref{eq:factors}, the capture applies a convenient power-of-two
scale rather than the exact loss-averaging constant; this leaves every factor
unchanged and is the reason diagnostics that report $B$ in summed-loss units
must rescale.

Output moments, their roots and the corresponding momentum are sharded by
contiguous layer ownership, four layers per rank. Per-update, each rank
accumulates replicated packed Gram partials, a \verb|reduce_scatter_tensor|
delivers globally pooled sums to each layer's owner, the owner computes its
roots and its four matrices' updates, and parameter deltas are all-gathered so
every replica applies all 224 updates. The output statistics run in a second
instance of the root engine with its own CUDA side stream, NCCL communicator
and host refresh pool. All of this is work the baseline does not do, which is
precisely why Section~\ref{sec:null} insists on a control that does it too.

\subsection{The arms}

Each arm is a choice of which factors in \eqref{eq:family} are applied, and at
what richness. Table~\ref{tab:arms} defines the ones we ran. Three design axes
matter and are varied independently.

\textbf{Which side.} Arms either \emph{add} output factors to the input-side
baseline (keeping $R,\hat R$), or \emph{replace} the input factors with output
ones ($\leftall$, and $\texttt{down\_hybrid}$ on the down projections only).
The replacement arms are the ones that test whether the output side can stand
alone, and they also shrink the optimizer: replacing the down projection's
width-2048 input moment with its width-512 output moment is 16$\times$ less
moment storage and 64$\times$ less nominal dense root work for that layer.

\textbf{Which richness.} A factor can be the dense spectral root of
\eqref{eq:factors}, the same exponent applied elementwise to the moment's
diagonal, or absent. The diagonal variant is not merely cheaper: because
elementwise powers need no eigendecomposition, it is computed \emph{exactly from
the current update's moment} every update, whereas a dense root is produced by a
cached-eigenbasis backend with repair rounds, exact fallbacks, and asynchronous
host refresh every four updates. Richness and freshness are therefore coupled
in the arms as we ran them, which is a limitation we confront directly in
Section~\ref{sec:freshness}.

\textbf{Which breadth.} The first wave applied output factors to the down
projections alone (32 of 224 matrices, 12.6\% of trainable parameters); later
waves applied them to all seven projections. Because the narrow and broad
versions also differ in their root backend, we do not read a difference between
them as a pure breadth effect.

\subsection{Two controls that the design forces}
\label{sec:method-controls}

\paragraph{The matched null.}
Adding output factors to this implementation changes the numerical path for
every block matrix: the baseline uses a batched update with fused
direction/decay products and split-precision root products, while the
output-side arms use a per-matrix path with direct FP32 products and separately
formed direction and decay. Momentum is bitwise identical, but the applied
delta differs by floating-point reassociation, and the host refresh schedule
changes because there are now two root engines competing for the same two
worker threads. A loss difference between an output-side arm and the baseline
therefore confounds geometry with execution.

The matched null resolves this. It runs the candidate's entire code path ---
cotangent capture, pooled moments, communication, root computation, host
refresh, scheduling, checkpoint state --- and then forces $L=\hat L=I$. Its
target is the baseline's mathematics via the candidate's execution. Candidate
minus null isolates geometry; null minus baseline measures the path.
Section~\ref{sec:null} reports that the second quantity is not small.

\paragraph{The latency variant.}
Output rows exist only after the backward pass, so the implementation launches
output statistics and roots after the last microbatch's backward and then waits
for them inside the same update. That wait is on the critical path. The lagged
arms apply factors computed from the \emph{previous} update's rows, moving
collection to just before the next launch and giving the work a full update plus
a forward and backward to complete. At $\beta=0.938$ a one-update lag is about
one-sixteenth of the averaging window, far less staleness than Shampoo-family
methods accept, but it is still a change to the update and not a pure
efficiency control: candidate-minus-lagged measures what the staleness costs,
and the pair together separate latency from geometry.

\subsection{Where the cost is}
\label{sec:method-cost}

The output side's expense is concentrated and predictable. Of the 224 output
statistics, 160 are at width 512 and 64 at width 2048. Dense root work scales as
$d^3$ and Gram accumulation as $d^2$, so the two MLP-expansion projections carry
96.2\% of the eigendecomposition work and 86.5\% of the Gram work while being
29\% of the statistics. They hold 0.500 of the 0.579\,GiB of packed Gram
partials and 1.000 of the 1.156\,GiB of cached eigenbases. Per rank, an arm with
dense output factors on all blocks adds roughly 0.58\,GiB of replicated Gram
partials, an equal \verb|reduce_scatter| payload per update, about 1.8\,GiB of
BF16 sampled-row buffers, and 0.75\,GiB of owned output state.

This is why the arm family includes three different ways to cheapen the output
side rather than one, and why the width-2048 factors are the natural first
target: \texttt{inner\_outer\_hidden} removes them, \mlpdiag{} diagonalizes
them, and \alldiag{} diagonalizes everything, removing output
eigendecomposition entirely. Newton--Schulz remains the dominant per-matrix
cost in every arm, so no arm on this path can be faster than a Muon-form update
on the same path; the savings available are savings against the dense output
arms, not against the baseline.
