TL;DR

We introduce Toner1, a general preconditioning rule that uses each layer’s input statistics. Applied to Muon and AdamW as MuonT and AdamT, it lowers validation loss in language-model pretraining, especially at larger batches.
We find that the inputs to transformer layers are strongly anisotropic: activations have much larger components along some directions than others. This observation motivated the development of Toner.
Our experiments show improvements over Muon across 130M–1.2B models, multiple batch sizes, and 1×–8× Chinchilla training budgets for the 130M model. On 1.2B models, Toner achieves estimated token savings of up to 22% at the same loss while adding only about 1.5% to total training time. Our results also suggest that MuonT can roughly double Muon’s critical batch size in some of the settings we study.

3.2Decoupling the Inner and Outer Strengths

The coordinate construction uses the same transformation for both factors, but they affect the update differently. In the ideal full-column-rank case, the inner factor changes the orientations of the update’s responses to input directions, while the outer factor sets their lengths. We suspected that gradient noise could affect their useful strengths differently: orientation changes might remain helpful at smaller batches even when strong rescaling gives too much weight to noisy directions. We use (½, ⅛) as a fixed configuration across our main experiments and recommend it as a robust default for practical use.6

4Results
4.1Setup and Evaluation

We evaluate MuonT on Marin's open Llama-style models [9, 2]: the 130M, 300M, 520M and 1.2B models. Our main metric is final cross-entropy loss on held-out C4 text from the Paloma suite [10]. At each model size, the batch-size comparisons use the same architecture, training data and token budget—about 20 tokens per non-embedding parameter [11].11

The GitHub repository includes the hyperparameters and reproduction instructions for our main Marin and nanoGPT experiments.

We start from Marin's tuned Muon settings. For the 130M–520M models, we use Dang et al.'s transfer rules [12] to choose initial settings at larger batches, then refine the baseline by varying one hyperparameter at a time. The 1.2B model comparison uses transferred settings with a smaller baseline search.12

For the scaling and longer-training comparisons, we emphasize that MuonT applies a fixed rule to the base Muon configurations, using the same strengths and learning-rate multiplier from Section 3.3 across these comparisons. We do not run a separate MuonT hyperparameter search at each setting, so additional tuning could yield further gains.

4.2Scaling Across Models and Batches

MuonT lowers validation loss across 130M–1.2B models, with larger gains at larger batch sizes (Figure 1). This batch-size trend is consistent with Zhang et al.'s analysis [13]: the benefits of preconditioning can become more pronounced as larger batches reduce gradient noise.

At the smallest tested batches, the two methods reach similar losses. At a 4M-token batch, the estimated token savings range from 13–24% across the four model sizes. For the 1.2B model, the estimate is about 22% at 8M. These savings measure the improvement over Muon at the same batch size.

The larger gains at large batches suggest that Toner mitigates some of the optimization cost of large-batch training. The savings at a fixed batch size should therefore not be read as a model-size trend. Figure 10 explores how batch size and training length affect the gains in our earlier (¼, ¼) experiments on the 130M model.13

AdamT, using the (¼, ¼) factors introduced in Section 3.3, also lowers validation loss relative to tuned AdamW in the Marin comparisons (Figure 1, AdamW view). At a 2M-token batch, the estimated token savings are about 24% for the 130M model and 15% for the 300M model.14

We also use the modded-nanoGPT setup of Dang et al. [12], training a 162M model on FineWeb and comparing against their published baselines, tuned separately at each batch size.15 MuonT’s advantage over Muon again grows with batch size (Figure 11 in Appendix A4). We keep MuonT’s strengths fixed and select a learning rate at each batch from the grids in Section 3.2.

4.3Training Longer

MuonT retains a loss advantage when we train the Marin 130M model for up to eight times its base budget of 2.7B tokens, reaching 21.5B tokens. Prior work observes that critical batch size grows approximately with the square root of the number of training tokens [15, 16]. We follow this scaling when increasing the training budget, using a 1M-token batch at the 1× budget and increasing it to about 2.8M tokens at 8×.16 We re-tune Muon at each budget and keep MuonT’s strengths and learning-rate transfer rule fixed.

Under power-law loss scaling, a similar percentage saving in tokens corresponds to a smaller absolute loss gap at longer training budgets. Here, the loss gap narrows from about 0.024 to 0.012 nats while estimated token savings remain around 10–13% (Figure 4).

4.4Larger Batches and Fewer Updates

The fixed-token loss-versus-batch curves in Figure 1 suggest that MuonT can roughly double Muon’s critical batch size in some of the settings we study. For example, the 1.2B model reaches a final C4 loss of 2.8936 with MuonT at a 4M-token batch, compared with 2.8986 for Muon at a 2M-token batch, using the same training budget.

With enough parallel hardware, a larger batch can process the same tokens in fewer sequential updates and shorten training, provided it does not require more tokens to reach the target loss [17]. We thus test whether MuonT can use a larger batch while keeping the token budget and final loss comparable.

For this comparison, we train the Marin 130M model with both optimizers on the same 2.7B tokens, using a 0.5M-token batch for Muon and a 0.75M-token batch for MuonT. The larger batch uses one third fewer updates—3,413 instead of 5,120—while reaching a slightly lower final C4 loss: 3.4571 versus 3.4621.

Critical batch size can grow rapidly early in training [18], motivating a smaller initial batch followed by an increase. We therefore repeat the comparison with batch sizes that gradually increase during this phase. MuonT again uses two-thirds as many updates, with final loss about 0.0016 nats lower (Figure 5).17

Geometry and the steepest-descent interpretation
5.2Input Energy, Curvature, and Noise

Changing output orientations and rescaling response lengths both improve training in the preceding experiments. To relate these changes to the loss, our earlier (¼, ¼) studies measured curvature along input directions. At the examined checkpoints, directional curvature grows roughly in proportion to input energy (Figure 13). In these probes, high-energy inputs correspond to greater loss sensitivity, supporting smaller responses along those directions.

Since Toner’s advantage grows with batch size, we also study whether it depends on which input directions carry gradient noise. In our earlier (¼, ¼) study at a 2M-token batch, adding noise to gradient components associated with low-energy inputs reduces Toner’s loss advantage over Muon by about 79%. Adding noise in high-energy directions increases its relative advantage. Both optimizers reach higher absolute losses under every added-noise condition (Figure 13, noise view). Where the noise is added matters, but these earlier studies do not yet explain MuonT’s batch-size dependence.

5.3Weight Decay and Learning Rate
Separating Outer Scaling from Weight Decay

Our recipe uses the outer factor to shape both the update and weight decay. Changing its strength therefore changes two things at once, leaving unclear how much each contributes to the gain. We vary outer scaling and decay separately, with the inner strength fixed at ½, to distinguish their effects (Figure 7).

With the MuonT adaptive update fixed, shaped decay gives lower loss than plain decay at all four tested batches. With the decay root fixed at ⅛, retaining outer scaling improves loss at three of the four batches; omitting it is slightly better at 512K. Changing decay alone therefore does not account for all the benefit of the outer factor.

Takeaway

Using layer inputs to construct updates improves language-model training, especially at larger batches. Our experiments suggest that making feature changes more even is only part of the story: changing update orientations also helps, and the inner and outer factors benefit from different strengths. We recommend MuonT with (½, ⅛) strengths and shaped weight decay as a robust practical default across the settings we tested. Our nanoGPT comparisons also suggest that (¾, ⅛) may give better results at larger batches. Why MuonT’s advantage grows with batch size remains an open question.
